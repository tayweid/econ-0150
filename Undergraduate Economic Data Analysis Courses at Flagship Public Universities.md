## Undergraduate Economic Data Analysis Courses at Flagship Public Universities

## Executive Summary

Undergraduate economics programs at many flagship and large public universities now offer specialized **data analysis courses** distinct from the traditional theoretical econometrics courses. These courses, often geared toward Bachelor of Arts (B.A.) students or those pursuing applied economics, emphasize practical data-handling skills and empirical analysis over formal proofs. They complement or serve as alternatives to the more mathematically intensive econometrics classes (often favored by Bachelor of Science or quantitatively-focused students). Key characteristics of these data analysis courses include:

- **Practical Orientation:** The focus is on using real economic data to answer questions, including data acquisition, cleaning, visualization, and interpretation. For example, the University of North Carolina’s Econ 400 is a “comprehensive introduction” to statistics and econometrics that highlights hands-on data analysis using statistical software.
- **Distinct from Econometrics:** Unlike traditional econometrics courses that delve into the statistical theory (e.g. derivations of estimators, proofs of assumptions), these courses stress *application*. At UC Berkeley, for instance, the standard econometrics course is offered in two flavors: Econ 140 (applied econometrics) and Econ 141 (math-intensive econometrics). Econ 140 (often taken by B.A. students) uses statistical techniques and software to “arrive at the right conclusion” from data with less emphasis on derivations, whereas Econ 141 provides a more theoretical treatment. This bifurcation illustrates how institutions tailor the depth of theory to student needs.
- **Common Learning Goals:** Students are expected to **master statistical tools and empirical methods** to analyze economic data. Typical learning outcomes include the ability to *summarize and visualize data, estimate econometric models (like regressions), and critically interpret the results*. Many courses require projects or empirical exercises. For example, the University of Illinois’ two-course sequence (Econ 202 & 203) uses a “practical project format” to teach students how to apply statistics to real-life economic data and how to identify appropriate techniques to solve problems. Likewise, the University of Florida’s Econ 3430 introduces students to large economic datasets and methods of cleaning and visualizing data, giving “hands-on experience with economic data analysis”.
- **Tools and Software:** There is a strong emphasis on modern analytical software. Programs commonly use statistical packages like **R or Stata**, and sometimes Python or Excel, to implement analysis. R is frequently used in courses that blend data science with economics (UNC’s Econ 400 uses RStudio for statistical computing). Stata has been a mainstay in econometrics instruction (for example, UC Berkeley’s econometrics courses historically incorporated Stata tutorials). Python is emerging in some curricula (UC Berkeley’s new Econ 148 course focuses on data science skills in Python for economists). Introductory data analysis courses may even use Excel for simplicity; at UIUC, students in Econ 203 become “adept at…using Excel” to perform regressions and analysis.
- **Applied Skills vs. Theory:** Overall, these courses tilt toward **applied skills development**. They train students to handle real-world data issues (such as missing data, outliers, causal inference challenges) and to effectively communicate results, rather than proving theorems. For instance, the University of Wisconsin–Madison’s curriculum highlights learning to “download, clean, and analyze large economic datasets using modern statistical software” and to communicate findings professionally. Many programs underscore that economists spend a significant portion of research time on data preparation and diagnostics, which these courses reflect. By contrast, the more theoretical econometrics classes concentrate on statistical underpinnings (e.g. proofs of estimator properties, derivation of standard errors under various assumptions) and may require additional advanced math.

In summary, the landscape of undergraduate econ data analysis courses shows a clear trend: economics departments are placing greater importance on *empirical data skills*. Whether through dedicated courses or separate tracks, students can gain experience with statistical software, work with real datasets, and undertake applied projects. These courses are often part of a B.A. economics pathway or offered as electives/certificates for those interested in analytics. Meanwhile, the traditional econometrics courses remain for those who need deeper theoretical grounding (often B.S. majors or honors students), illustrating a dual approach to econometric education.

## School-by-School Profiles

### University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC)

UNC’s Economics B.A. program integrates data analysis early in the curriculum. **ECON 400 – Introduction to Data Science and Econometrics** is a required course that combines foundational statistics with an introduction to econometric methods. This course is distinctly *applied* in nature: it covers descriptive statistics, probability, hypothesis testing, and regression, but with an emphasis on practical implementation using software. The syllabus notes that students will use **R and RStudio** extensively for data work.

**Course Content & Framing:** ECON 400 is described as a “comprehensive introduction” to statistical analysis, including graphical data exploration and regression techniques, and it “includes a substantial introduction to basic econometrics”. The framing is skill-oriented – it is part of UNC’s general education (fulfilling a Quantitative Reasoning requirement) and aims to build data literacy. The learning outcomes stress real-world skills: summarizing and presenting quantitative data, constructing and assessing statistical models, and drawing conclusions from data. In other words, students learn *how to analyze economic data and interpret the results*, rather than just the theory behind the methods.

**Breadth & Depth:** As an introductory course, ECON 400 covers a broad range of topics but at a manageable depth. Students start with descriptive statistics and visualization, then progress through probability distributions to inferential statistics (estimation and hypothesis tests), and finally to simple and multiple regression analysis. The inclusion of multiple regression and possibly introductory econometric concepts means students get a taste of what an econometrics course entails, but without requiring advanced math (the prerequisites are just ECON 101 and a basic calculus or stats course). This breadth prepares students for more advanced electives, while the depth is sufficient for them to competently perform basic empirical research.

**Prerequisites:** As noted, the prerequisites are ECON 101 (Principles of Economics) and a calculus or introductory statistics course. This suggests the course is typically taken in the sophomore year. The calculus requirement ensures students have some mathematical maturity, but the course itself does not dive into calculus-based derivations – it’s more about application.

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of ECON 400, students should be able to **handle data end-to-end**: formulating an economic question, using data to answer it, and communicating the results. UNC explicitly lists outcomes like the ability to “compute representations of data,” assess models and their limitations, and “synthesize and present quantitative data to others”. These align with training students to be savvy consumers and producers of empirical analysis in economics.

**Software and Tools:** UNC’s course uses **R** as the primary tool. This choice reflects the course’s orientation towards modern, open-source data science tools. Through nine assignments (as mentioned in the syllabus), students get hands-on experience analyzing data in R, which likely involves data cleaning, running statistical tests, and creating visualizations. The instructor notes that assignments mix “analytical questions and empirical work” for which RStudio is used, reinforcing that the class is half theoretical, half practical. The use of R also positions students well for more advanced work or for jobs requiring data analysis, since they gain programming experience.

**Distinction from Econometrics Tracks:** UNC’s ECON 400 essentially *is* the first econometrics course for both the B.A. and B.S. tracks (both degrees require it). However, the B.S. in economics at UNC goes further: after ECON 400, B.S. students take advanced econometric or data analysis electives (like ECON 570, 571, etc., e.g., Machine Learning and Econometrics). The B.A. students might stop after ECON 400 or take only one additional applied elective. In effect, ECON 400 serves the applied needs of B.A. students by itself – it gives them enough econometric knowledge to understand empirical studies and do basic analyses – whereas it serves as a launchpad for B.S. students who will delve into more rigorous courses. Compared to a traditional econometrics course (which at some universities might assume more calculus or focus on proofs of OLS properties), ECON 400 is less theoretical. A UNC student noted on a forum that an alternative statistics course (STOR 155/Stat 101 equivalent) is “more chill” and project-based, whereas ECON 3720 (an older designation for econometrics) was “more theoretical in nature”. This implies that UNC’s approach balances theory and practice in ECON 400 but ensures that practice isn’t neglected – indeed, the course’s positioning in the curriculum and heavy R usage point to a decidedly applied emphasis.

**Sources:** UNC ECON 400 Syllabus; UNC Course Catalog.

### University of California, Berkeley

UC Berkeley’s economics department provides a clear example of bifurcating econometrics instruction into applied vs. theoretical paths. **Econ 140 – Econometrics** and **Econ 141 – Econometrics (Mathematical Intensive)** are two courses that cover similar core material but with different orientations. In addition, Berkeley has introduced **Econ 148 – Economics Data Science** (often titled “Data Analysis for Economists”), which focuses on data handling and computational skills complementary to econometrics.

**Course Content & Framing:** *Econ 140* is described in the catalog as an introduction to statistical analysis of economic data (econometrics). In practice, instructors emphasize using econometric methods on real data and interpreting the output. For example, one instructor’s overview for Econ 140 stated it focuses on applying linear regression models in various settings and includes topics like causal inference, with the goal of teaching students “how to arrive at the right conclusion using statistical techniques…given a cleaned dataset”. *Econ 141*, by contrast, covers the same econometric models but delves deeper into the linear algebra and statistical theory behind them (it’s intended for students comfortable with advanced math, such as those who have taken more than the minimum math requirements). The existence of these two courses signals an explicit framing: **Econ 140 is for the typical econ major (often B.A.) who needs practical skills**, whereas Econ 141 is for those considering graduate study or a more quantitative focus (akin to a B.S. track).

**Breadth & Depth:** Both 140 and 141 cover core econometrics topics: OLS regression, hypothesis testing, model specification issues, and possibly extensions like instrumental variables or panel data. The *breadth* is similar, but the *depth of theory* differs. In Econ 140, proofs and derivations are minimized; instead, students might spend more time in computer labs running regressions on datasets (Berkeley often uses examples from real economic studies). Econ 141 would engage in matrix algebra derivations of OLS, probability theory review, and proving the Gauss-Markov theorem, etc. The learning outcomes of Econ 140 are oriented toward understanding when and how to apply techniques and how to interpret econometric results in economic terms. For instance, one can expect assignments requiring students to write short reports on what an estimated model implies about an economic question, rather than deriving formulas.

**Prerequisites:** Berkeley requires intermediate economic theory and an intro statistics course prior to econometrics. In practice, students must complete either an intro econometrics/stat course (formerly Econ 140’s prerequisite was a stats class or Data 8). There’s also an option that Data Science majors who take Data 100 (an advanced data science course) can count that as an econometrics equivalent. For Econ 141, more math is expected (Math 54 – linear algebra – is typically recommended). The **split between 140 and 141** is often self-selected by students based on their math comfort; it’s not a formal separate track but a choice.

**Learning Objectives:** In *Econ 140*, the objectives are to be able to *conduct regression analysis on economic data and draw valid conclusions*. This involves learning to check the assumptions of models in practice (e.g., detecting heteroskedasticity), using software to estimate models, and interpreting statistical output (coefficients, R-squared, p-values) in context. Communication of findings is also emphasized; students often have to interpret results in plain language (for example, explaining the policy implication of a regression coefficient). In *Econ 141*, objectives include a stronger theoretical understanding – students should understand why OLS is BLUE (Best Linear Unbiased Estimator) under certain conditions, etc., in addition to doing what 140 students do.

**Software or Tools Used:** Berkeley’s econometrics courses incorporate statistical software. Historically, **Stata** was commonly used (Berkeley has an Econometrics Lab with Stata access). In fact, course resources for Econ 140 have included “Annotated Stata output” and Stata do-files for examples. However, in recent years some instructors have shifted to **R** for undergraduate econometrics, leveraging Berkeley’s broader initiative in data science. A Reddit discussion among Berkeley students noted: “140 provides much more examples and uses R and isn’t nearly as math intensive,” whereas 141 is more proof-based. This suggests that at least in some iterations, Econ 140 teaches students to use R for regression analysis, giving them practical coding experience. Meanwhile, Econ 148 (the data science for economists course) uses **Python (pandas, Jupyter notebooks)** extensively. In Econ 148, students learn how to gather data (via APIs, web sources), clean and merge datasets, and perform exploratory data analysis and visualization in Python. This is an entirely hands-on course, and it explicitly does *not* focus on regression modeling theory – instead, it complements 140/141 by covering the 80% of effort economists spend *before* the regression: getting the data in shape. The presence of Econ 148 indicates Berkeley’s recognition that data skills (like programming and data wrangling) are crucial alongside classical econometric training.

**Distinction from Econometrics Track:** Berkeley’s approach clearly delineates the applied vs. theoretical learning. A departmental advisory might be: if you’re a typical econ major, take 140 (and perhaps 148 if you want more data science skills); if you’re going to grad school in econ or doing the honors thesis, take 141 (for rigorous theory) and maybe also 140 or an advanced metrics course. The difference is formalized by separate course numbers. Moreover, **the content of Econ 148 is distinct from both 140 and 141** – while 140/141 assume you have clean data and focus on analysis, 148 teaches how to get that clean data and do reproducible research (one syllabus notes economists spend ~1/3 of research time on data cleaning, which this class addresses). In summary, Berkeley provides a spectrum: pure data handling (148, very applied), standard applied econometrics (140), and theoretical econometrics (141). This allows students to customize their preparation according to their goals.

**Sources:** Berkeley Econ 148 Syllabus; Berkeley Course Catalog and materials.

### University of Florida (UF)

The University of Florida’s economics department has developed offerings that highlight applied data analysis skills, notably through an **Econometric and Data Analysis Certificate** and courses like **ECO 3430 – Economic Analysis of Data**. In the standard B.A. Economics, students are required to take an introductory statistics course (STA 2023) and an econometrics course (ECO 4421). ECO 3430 is an *additional* course that appears aimed at giving students more hands-on experience with data prior to or alongside econometrics.

**Course Content & Framing:** **ECO 3430 – Economic Analysis of Data** is described as an introduction to “prominent big economic datasets” and to common practices in data cleaning and visualization. The framing is clearly applied: it’s about working with real-world data (the mention of *big datasets* suggests maybe Census data, Fed economic data, etc.) and about practical issues like inconsistencies in data. This is distinct from a traditional stats or metrics course which might assume a tidy dataset is given. By teaching data cleaning, UF is signaling to students that handling raw data is a key skill. Visualization tools are also mentioned, indicating students likely learn to plot and present data (possibly using software like Excel, R, or Python libraries).

This course is likely part of UF’s **certificate in Econometric and Data Analysis**, which “aims to develop competence with highly employable data analytic skills” and specifically encourages economics majors to take it. It is not a substitute for econometrics but a complement. In fact, UF’s curriculum includes **ECO 4421 – Econometrics** (an applied econometrics course focusing on regression and policy evaluation) and **ECO 4422 – Econometrics 2** (advanced topics like causal inference methods). Those are more akin to traditional econometrics I and II. ECO 3430 sits earlier in the pipeline: its prerequisites are only principles of micro and macro, and an intro stats class, meaning students can take it relatively early, before tackling 4421.

**Breadth & Depth:** ECO 3430 covers a breadth of practical data topics: how to obtain data, how to detect errors or anomalies in data, basic descriptive and visual analysis, etc. It likely does some basic statistical analysis too, but not at the depth of full econometric modeling. The description doesn’t explicitly mention running regressions; it emphasizes preparation and exploration of data. This suggests that the depth is in *data handling techniques* rather than statistical theory. Students might, for example, learn how to use Excel or a statistical package to merge datasets, create charts, compute summary statistics, and maybe run a simple correlation or trend analysis. The course is 4 credits, implying a substantive amount of content and lab work.

**Prerequisites:** Principles of Microeconomics and Macroeconomics (ECO 2013 & 2023) and a statistics course (STA 2023) are prerequisites. This means students already have basic economic reasoning and basic understanding of statistical concepts like mean, variance, maybe confidence intervals, when they enter. With that, ECO 3430 can skip re-teaching mean and go straight into applied matters like “here’s how you calculate those in software and interpret them on real data.” The stats prereq ensures students know the fundamentals of inference, so the course can focus on the *execution* and not have to derive formulas.

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of ECO 3430, students should be able to **take a raw dataset and produce meaningful analysis**. Specifically, they should know how to identify data errors or outliers, clean the data set (e.g., deal with missing values or inconsistent units), create informative visualizations (like plotting economic time series or distributions), and perform basic analysis to answer economic questions. The course likely improves their proficiency in software and their understanding of data documentation. Because it’s part of a certificate highlighting “highly employable data analytic skills”, one objective is to make students comfortable with tasks an economic analyst might do in a job (like working with GDP or labor statistics data).

**Software/Tools:** The course description mentions “various software packages” for visualization. While it doesn’t list them explicitly, plausible tools include **Excel** (commonly taught for basic data analysis and quick charts), **Stata or R** (since those are used in follow-on econometrics courses). It might expose students to more than one tool: for instance, using Excel for simple tasks and perhaps a stat package for more complex tasks. The inclusion of data visualization suggests maybe even introducing Tableau or Python’s matplotlib/seaborn, but in an econ undergrad context, that’s less likely. Given that UF’s Econ department emphasizes data analysis, they might use **R** in 3430 to ease students into coding before hitting econometrics. Also, **GEO 4169 – Geographic Information Systems** is required in the certificate, meaning UF encourages multidisciplinary tool usage (GIS software exposure, etc.). In any case, by the end students have seen practical software usage beyond calculators.

**Distinction from Econometrics Track:** ECO 3430 is distinct in that it *does not* emphasize econometric modeling or economic theory, but rather the preparatory and exploratory stages of empirical work. In contrast, **ECO 4421 (Econometrics)** at UF covers regression techniques and how to estimate and evaluate economic relationships, with an emphasis on practical use of econometrics for policy analysis. That course requires calculus and intermediate theory, and delves into issues of causal inference and modeling. ECO 3430 requires none of that and can be taken by students earlier; it’s likely less rigorous mathematically. The department allows economics majors to count ECO 3430 toward their major electives, showing it values this practical training. The Econometric and Data Analysis Certificate (which is open to all majors) includes ECO 3430, 4421, 4422, and a GIS course – combining theory and application. The certificate’s description emphasizes the *mix* of skills: microeconomic theory, statistics, and “hands-on experience analyzing real data” (similar wording appears in UF’s goals).

In sum, at UF the presence of ECO 3430 signals a commitment to applied data skills for undergrads. It ensures that by the time a student finishes econometrics, they not only understand regressions but also how to manage and visualize data on their own – a combination highly valued in policy analysis and business analytics.

### University of Wisconsin–Madison

UW–Madison’s economics program offers both standard and applied routes for econometric training, integrated with a **Data Analytics** emphasis. Notably, Wisconsin has an **Economic Analytics Certificate** (for non-majors) and courses like **ECON 400 – Introduction to Applied Econometrics** and **ECON 410 – Introductory Econometrics**, as well as **ECON 570 – Fundamentals of Data Analytics for Economists**. These reflect a layered approach to teaching data analysis: a core course that can be taken in an applied or theoretical version, and advanced electives focusing on data science techniques.

**Course Content & Framing:** **ECON 400 – Introduction to Applied Econometrics** is geared toward using econometric methods in practice. It “guides students in the use and interpretation of economic data” and addresses common issues in empirical work. The phrase “applied econometrics” signals that this course likely spends significant time on practical modeling (using real datasets, interpreting regression output, diagnosing problems like multicollinearity or heteroskedasticity) rather than on theory proofs. Meanwhile, **ECON 410 – Introductory Econometrics** presumably covers similar material with more emphasis on derivations and statistical theory. Both are considered “Core ECON courses” for the major, and students can choose either to fulfill their econometrics requirement.

UW–Madison also introduced **ECON 570 – Fundamentals of Data Analytics for Economists**, which is a data-focused course. This course is described as an introduction to “the data that underlies quantitative economic analysis” and teaches how to formulate research questions, access data, conduct analysis, and report findings. It sounds like a blend of an empirical research methods course and a computing course. ECON 570 and a topics course (ECON 695 – Topics in Economic Data Analysis) are part of electives available for a data analytics focus.

**Breadth & Depth:** In **ECON 400**, breadth includes simple and multiple regression, possibly limited dependent variable models, and the standard toolkit of an introductory econometrics course. Depth is in the application: students might do a small research project or at least extensive homework using data. They learn how to handle violations of assumptions in a practical way (e.g., what to do if errors are heteroskedastic – probably learn to use robust standard errors, but without deeply proving the math of it). **ECON 410**, by contrast, might assign proofs or derivations, giving more depth in theory per topic.

**ECON 570** has an interesting breadth: it covers the *research process* (question to conclusion) and specific types of data (panel data like the Panel Study of Income Dynamics, time series like National Income accounts). It also explicitly teaches “modern tools to effectively manage data and perform analysis”. This means the course likely involves programming (perhaps in R or Python) and deals with practical issues such as data scraping or combining data sources. The depth here is more on technical data skills (like writing code to transform data) rather than advanced econometric theory.

**Prerequisites:** UW–Madison requires an intermediate stats course before these. For example, prerequisites for ECON 400/410 include Econ 310 (Statistics: Measurement in Economics) or an equivalent statistics sequence. Also required is intermediate micro (Econ 301). This ensures students come in knowing basic statistical inference and economic reasoning. For ECON 570, the prereqs are similar (stats and intermediate micro), making it an upper-level course typically taken after the core theory and stats are done. Essentially, students do theory courses, a stats course, then they have options: take 400 (applied metrics) or 410 (metrics) and possibly 570 (data analytics) if they want more empirical skill.

**Learning Objectives:** For **ECON 400**, the objectives are to be able to *apply* econometric techniques to real economic data, to understand the potential problems that can arise, and to interpret results for economic policy or business decisions. The course description emphasizes using and interpreting data and solving methodological problems. So, a student finishing 400 should be comfortable running a regression in software, checking the residuals, explaining whether an economic theory is supported by the data or not, etc. In fact, the **Economic Analytics Certificate** learning outcomes include being able to “evaluate the extent” to which conditions for causal inference hold and to “download, clean, and analyze large economic datasets using modern software” – skills mirrored in these courses. **ECON 570** has explicit outcomes such as understanding the research process and communicating quantitative ideas with figures. So the focus is also on communication and end-to-end research ability, not just on doing a calculation. That course aims to produce students who can initiate an empirical project (find data, analyze it, and present it). This is a skill often expected at the senior thesis level; UW is embedding parts of it in a junior-level class.

**Software/Tools:** UW’s courses definitely incorporate software. **Stata** has traditionally been used in econometrics courses; however, given the mention of “modern tools” and that ECON 570 is in the data science certificate, **R or Python** might be used, at least in ECON 570. The Economic Analytics certificate is listed under Letters & Science, possibly aligning with the campus-wide data science curriculum that often uses R/Python. Even in ECON 400/410, students likely use a statistical package for homework (regression by hand is impractical for real data). There is also an **ECON 421 (Economic Decision Analysis)** cross-listed with Ag Econ, which explicitly uses spreadsheets extensively for applied analyses, indicating UW is not averse to Excel where appropriate.

**Distinction from Econometrics Tracks:** UW–Madison’s structure basically offers **ECON 400 vs ECON 410** as parallel options – one applied, one more theoretical – similar to Berkeley’s 140/141 model. In fact, the Guide lists both under core options and labels Econ 400 as “Applied Econometrics”. This allows B.A. students or those less mathematically inclined to choose the applied version without matrix algebra, while students eyeing grad school might choose 410 for a rigorous approach (or even take both). The **ECON 570 and 695** courses add further applied training. These are not required for the major, but for those interested (possibly taken by honors students or those doing the certificate). They highlight areas beyond standard econometrics: 570 (data analytics) gives computing and data-wrangling savvy; 695 (Topics in Economic Data Analysis) might be a seminar where students read and critique empirical papers, learning research design and advanced methods. That *broadens* the skill set from just “run a regression” to “design a strategy to answer a question and implement it with data,” which is a distinguishing aspect of an applied track versus purely theory.

In summary, UW–Madison’s program reflects a **comprehensive approach**: required knowledge of econometrics (with an option to emphasize application), plus optional courses for deeper data analysis skills. The learning goals emphasize not just performing econometric techniques but doing so in a way that mirrors real research, including data preparation and communication of results. It ensures that an econ graduate from UW can both understand the theory *and* handle practical data challenges, depending on the path they choose.

### University of Tennessee, Knoxville (UTK)

### University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign (UIUC)

The University of Illinois’ economics program (in the College of Liberal Arts & Sciences) structures data analysis training through a two-course sequence in statistics for economists, followed by optional advanced econometrics courses. The key undergraduate courses are **ECON 202 – Economic Statistics I** and **ECON 203 – Economic Statistics II**, which together fulfill the role of an applied data analysis foundation distinct from a purely theoretical econometrics class. Beyond those, UIUC offers **ECON 471 – Intro to Applied Econometrics** and other 400-level econometric electives for students who want more depth (especially those in the B.S. Econometrics & Quantitative Economics major).

**Course Content & Framing:** **ECON 202 and 203** are framed as a **business/economic statistics sequence with a practical orientation**. ECON 202 introduces basic statistical concepts (data presentation, descriptive stats, probability, distributions, estimation, hypothesis testing) with an approach that includes real-world application: the class uses a “practical project format” to reinforce learning. The idea is that students don’t just learn formulas, they apply them to data sets and scenarios. Indeed, the catalog explicitly states that 202 teaches concepts and how to apply them in "real-life" situations.

ECON 203 then continues this approach: it “builds upon” 202’s inferential statistics and focuses on regression analysis (simple and multiple linear regression) and time series techniques. It also uses a practical project format, aiming to extend the skill set to these econometric methods. Importantly, the description of Econ 203 emphasizes outcomes like identifying the proper statistical technique for a problem, using Excel to find answers, and interpreting results correctly. This framing is highly applied; it’s about doing analysis and making sense of it, not about mathematical proofs. In many programs, regression would be taught in an “Econometrics” class, but UIUC introduces it in this second course of the stats sequence, signaling that at the undergrad level they treat regression as an extension of statistics.

**Breadth & Depth:** Across 202 and 203, the breadth covers essentially an intro stats curriculum plus an intro econometrics curriculum. Students see everything from basic descriptive measures to correlation, confidence intervals, then OLS regression and possibly ARIMA basics for time series. The depth in theory is kept moderate. For example, they will learn the formulas and how to calculate a regression line, maybe even some idea of why least squares is used, but the emphasis is on understanding and using the results rather than deriving properties. The depth in application, however, is significant: UIUC requires that students do projects, meaning they must engage deeply with data analysis tasks. They might analyze a dataset in parts throughout the semester, or do case studies. The mention of time series in 203 suggests some depth in a specialized area (forecasting or trend analysis), but likely focusing on using a simple model to make forecasts rather than deriving complex time series theory.

**Prerequisites:** Calculus (MATH 220 or 221) is a prerequisite for Econ 202, and Econ 202 is a prerequisite for Econ 203. The calculus requirement ensures students can handle concepts like maximization or understanding functions/probabilities, but the course itself might only lightly use calculus (mostly summation notation and maybe partial derivatives in formulas). It is common because sometimes one might derive an expectation or do differentiation in an optimization context for stats (though minimal in intro stats). The key is that by the time they reach Econ 203, they have a full semester of stats behind them, so they are prepared for regression. Also, intermediate micro (ECON 302) is listed as a co-requisite or prerequisite for Econ 471, but not needed for 202/203 (these are taken typically sophomore year alongside intermediate theory).

**Learning Objectives:** After completing ECON 202 and 203, a student should be able to: (1) handle data sets (likely small to moderate size) in Excel or similar, (2) compute and interpret statistical summaries (mean, variance, correlations), (3) conduct hypothesis tests (e.g., test if a mean is different from zero, test if a regression coefficient is significant), (4) estimate and use regression models for basic prediction or inference, (5) check assumptions in an informal way (maybe see a residual plot), and (6) present the results of an analysis in a coherent way. The catalog explicitly says students will be trained to *identify the proper technique*, *find answers using Excel*, and *properly interpret results*. This triad – choose method, execute in software, interpret output – encapsulates the applied goals. Essentially, UIUC wants their B.A. econ graduates to be able to do an empirical project at an introductory level. Indeed, one of the learning outcomes for the BA is to “apply empirical evidence… using appropriate statistical techniques, and interpret the results”.

Because UIUC also has a more quantitative economics B.S., students aiming for that will go further. They might take **ECON 471 – Intro to Applied Econometrics**, which is a more advanced one-semester course focusing on the econometric theory-applications interplay (it requires Econ 203 and intermediate theory as prerequisites). Econ 471 likely ups the rigor slightly (maybe uses matrix algebra for multiple regression, and covers topics like instrumental variables or limited dependent variables that 203 wouldn’t). But it’s telling that it’s called “Applied Econometrics” and emphasizes the interplay between theory and economic applications. This indicates that even at the higher level, UIUC keeps the focus on linking to real data problems rather than pure theory. Then there are specialized courses like ECON 474 (Econometrics of Policy Evaluation) and ECON 475 (Forecasting) which align with what other schools do in separate electives. Those are likely taken by the more advanced students.

**Software/Tools:** UIUC explicitly uses **Excel** in Econ 203. Excel is likely chosen because it’s accessible and enough for small regression models (the Analysis ToolPak in Excel can do regressions). Using Excel also means less of a learning curve for non-specialist students, allowing them to focus on concepts. However, Excel has limitations, so it’s likely that in Econ 471 or the advanced courses, UIUC introduces Stata or R. The department’s website suggests that they encourage students, especially in the Econometrics & Quantitative Econ major, to learn statistical programming. But for the required BA sequence, Excel suffices. The learning goal of being “adept at finding answers… using Excel” indicates that by the end of 203, students can manage data and run analyses in a spreadsheet environment. They can probably do things like run a regression of, say, consumption on income using Excel and interpret the coefficients.

**Distinction from Econometrics Track:** For a typical econ B.A. student at UIUC, taking Econ 202 and 203 might be all the formal training in data analysis they get. These courses already incorporate regression, so such a student does get an “econometrics lite” training, but very applied. They might not take Econ 471 unless they want more (it’s not required for the BA). In contrast, a student in the B.S. Econometrics major or someone particularly interested in analytics will take Econ 471 and possibly the other 400-level electives. In essence, UIUC ensures all econ majors can handle and analyze data, and then provides optional courses to deepen those skills theoretically and technically for those who need it.

Because 202/203 are called “Economic Statistics” rather than “Econometrics,” students probably perceive them as part of their fundamental toolkit, not as something only theoreticians need. This naming and framing help distinguish them from any intimidating “econometrics” label, even though 203 covers a lot of what an econometrics course does. ### University of Texas at Austin (UT Austin)

UT Austin’s economics undergraduate program ensures students gain empirical analysis skills through a required statistics course and an econometrics course. While UT doesn’t explicitly label separate “applied” vs “theoretical” econometrics courses, the structure of **ECO 329 – Economic Statistics** followed by **ECO 341K – Introduction to Econometrics** serves to cover both applied data analysis fundamentals and the core regression techniques. This two-step sequence naturally distinguishes the general data analysis phase (329) from the econometric modeling phase (341K).

**Course Content & Framing:** **ECO 329 – Economic Statistics** is essentially an applied statistics course tailored for economics. It covers the interpretation of economic data and an introduction to statistical models, estimation, and inference specifically in an economics context. The content likely includes descriptive statistics, probability distributions, sampling theory, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests – in other words, core statistical inference topics, much like UIUC’s Econ 202. The description “interpretation of economic data” suggests the course emphasizes understanding what the statistical results mean for economic questions (for example, interpreting the result of a t-test in terms of economic significance). The framing is that this is a **toolbox course**: giving students the statistical foundation they need to later do regressions in 341K. It’s an upper-division course at UT, which is a bit unusual (many universities have stats as lower-division), but UT’s prerequisites enforce that students take intermediate micro (ECO 420K) before 329. This implies UT wants students to have theoretical context when analyzing data – possibly to be able to motivate statistical analysis with economic models.

**ECO 341K – Introduction to Econometrics** is framed as the follow-on where students learn regression analysis (the syllabus snippet from the catalog: “parameter estimation and inference in regression models” sums it up). It likely covers the classical linear regression model in detail, including assumptions, how to estimate (least squares), how to conduct hypothesis tests on coefficients, and common violations of assumptions. It may also introduce basic extensions like dummy variables, perhaps heteroskedasticity fixes, etc. The word “Introduction” indicates it’s a first course in econometrics, not extremely advanced but enough for undergrads to handle empirical work.

**Breadth & Depth:** In **ECO 329**, the breadth is general statistical methods (it might go up to simple regression or correlation at the end, but the heavy regression is left for 341K). Depth is likely moderate in calculation and concepts but light on derivations. Students probably practice computing probabilities, z-scores, t-tests, maybe chi-square tests, etc., and interpreting them. Given it’s a one-semester course, it covers quite a lot but at an introductory depth. UT might also include some econometrics preview in 329 (maybe one lecture on “what is regression” to segue into 341K).

In **ECO 341K**, the breadth is focused on regression and related econometric topics. It likely includes simple and multiple regression, the Gauss-Markov theorem (possibly stated but not proved in full), inference in regression, and problems like omitted variable bias, multicollinearity, etc. It might also cover a brief introduction to simultaneous equations or limited dep. vars if time permits, but often it’s mostly linear regression. The depth in theory would be medium: formulas for OLS are derived, and conditions for unbiasedness explained, but full proofs might not all be done (depending on the instructor). The depth in application is also significant: UT’s econometrics courses historically involve empirical assignments. The recommended text (Wooldridge’s “Introductory Econometrics”, per a syllabus reference) aligns with an applied approach using lots of examples. The course will have students run regressions (with software) and interpret output as part of homework or projects.

**Prerequisites:** As mentioned, UT requires **intermediate microeconomics (ECO 420K)** before taking ECO 329, which is a notable prerequisite not all schools have for their stats courses. Also required is calculus (implicitly, since intermediate micro at UT has a calculus prerequisite). For **ECO 341K**, the prerequisites are ECO 329 and ECO 420K (micro) with at least C-. In practice, students usually take 329 and 341K in sequence (commonly in junior year). The micro theory prerequisite ensures that by econometrics, students are familiar with economic models that generate the data they’ll analyze (for example, knowing what a demand curve is before running a regression on quantity vs. price data). This integration is pedagogically useful: students can better connect regression results to theoretical parameters.

**Learning Objectives:** For **ECO 329**, the objectives include understanding statistical reasoning and being able to apply statistical tests to economic data. A student should be able to compute and interpret a confidence interval (“Is the average income in our sample significantly different from the national average?” etc.) and test hypotheses (“Do the data provide evidence of an effect?”). They should also become literate in basic probability so they can follow econometric arguments. Essentially, after 329, students are prepared to ask: “How confident am I in this sample relationship? Could it be by chance?” and answer using statistical tools.

For **ECO 341K**, the primary goal is to enable students to **estimate econometric models and draw conclusions for economic questions**. By the end, they should be capable of taking an economic problem (like estimating the return to education on wages), specifying a regression model, using data to estimate it, and interpreting the coefficients (e.g., “each additional year of education is associated with X% higher wages, holding other factors constant”). They also learn to check issues – e.g., they might do a residual plot to detect heteroskedasticity or discuss why an omitted variable might bias their estimate. Another key learning outcome is understanding the concept of causality vs correlation – econometrics courses stress thinking about whether the regression coefficient reflects causation or if there’s endogeneity. UT’s course likely includes examples from real studies to illustrate these points, given Texas’s emphasis on policy relevant research in some tracks.

Additionally, since UT’s degree requirements note a minimum grade needed for ECO 329 and other core courses, the department clearly values mastery of this material.

**Software/Tools:** Historically, UT’s undergrad econometrics courses have used **Stata**. The reference to the Wooldridge textbook and various UT syllabi (and the presence of “.do files” or the like in course materials) suggest Stata is (or was) the primary tool. For example, UT instructors often require students to run regressions in Stata and interpret the output. There’s no direct mention in the catalog, but a **Scribd syllabus for ECO 341K** hints at Stata usage and quick guides to Stata are often provided to students. ECO 329 might not use much software beyond maybe Excel or a simple calculator for probability problems, but it could incorporate tools like **WebAssign** for homework (the search results mention a web-based homework lab used in 329). By econometrics (341K), certainly statistical software is in play, otherwise students cannot realistically do real-world regression assignments. The reliance on software underscores the applied nature: they’re not doing regressions by hand; they’re learning to use technology to analyze data – a critical skill.

**Distinction from Econometrics Tracks:** UT Austin doesn’t have separate “applied” vs “theory” econometrics courses, but the 329+341K sequence effectively separates the concerns. 329 is a prerequisite focusing on applied stats (with minimal theory beyond what’s needed), and 341K is an applied econometrics course that likely balances theory and empirical work. There might be an honors version or graduate level (like ECO 441K) that undergrads can take if they want more rigor – indeed, the BS in Economics option mentions 341K or 441K, where 441K might be a more advanced version. But for most, 341K suffices.

So, the **applied aspect** at UT is integrated: ECO 329 ensures students are comfortable with data interpretation and basic inference, which is a very applied skill (not abstract proof, but “tell me what this p-value means for this economic claim”). Then ECO 341K teaches them how to *build* those models and get results for more complex questions. In doing so, UT covers both bases – practical usage and understanding of assumptions – but always with an eye on interpretation. The stepwise nature ensures students aren’t overwhelmed with theory at once; they see value in stats via 329 (perhaps using examples like “does schooling affect earnings? Let’s see via a t-test/regression”) and then formalize it more in 341K.
